Expedition briefing

Antikythera

Between Kythera and Crete, on the sea lanes of the southern Aegean — a speck that punched above its weight.

Antikythera is a small Greek island (~21 km²) perched between the Peloponnese and Crete. Remote on a map, it sits on routes that linked the eastern and central Mediterranean for millennia.

That geography wrote an episodic history: Neolithic visitors with Melian obsidian, Bronze Age farms tied to Crete, a Hellenistic fortified community (often read as a pirate stronghold), Late Roman villages, thin Byzantine returns — and, offshore, the famous 1st-century BC wreck that yielded the Antikythera mechanism, the ancient geared “computer” that made the island’s name immortal.

The dig board you are playing uses the Antikythera Survey Project (Bevan & Conolly) — an intensive, island-wide surface survey that mapped tracts, terraces, geology, and finds across the whole rock. Your fogged squares are real survey units; the glory is in reading the landscape like an archaeologist.
